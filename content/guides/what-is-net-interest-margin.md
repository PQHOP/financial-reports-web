**Net interest margin (NIM)** is the gap between what a bank earns on the money it lends out or invests and what it pays for the money it borrowed, expressed as a percentage of its interest-earning assets (loans, securities, and similar holdings). It is the bank equivalent of a retailer's gross margin: the markup on the bank's core product, which is money itself.

## The formula

Net interest margin = net interest income / average interest-earning assets

**Net interest income** is interest earned on loans and securities minus interest paid on deposits and borrowed funds. A bank with $10 billion in interest-earning assets and $350 million of net interest income has a NIM of 3.5%.

## Why it moves

NIM is not a fixed number — it shifts with interest rates and with the bank's own choices:

- **The rate environment.** When central banks raise rates, a bank's loans usually reprice upward faster than the deposits funding them, which can widen NIM for a while — until deposit rates catch up. When rates fall, the reverse can happen.
- **Funding mix.** A bank funded mostly by low-cost checking and savings deposits has a lower cost of funds than one that relies on more expensive borrowed money or large time deposits (CDs), so its NIM tends to run higher for the same loan book.
- **Loan mix.** Credit cards and consumer loans typically carry much higher rates (and higher NIM) than mortgages or loans to large, low-risk corporate borrowers.
- **Competition for deposits.** When banks compete hard for deposits, they pay more for them, which compresses NIM even if loan rates stay flat.

## Why investors watch it

Net interest income is usually the largest single piece of a bank's revenue, so a small change in NIM — even a few tenths of a percentage point — can move profit by a lot more than the same percentage change would at a typical company. A bank that says revenue grew mainly "due to margin expansion" is describing a rate or funding-mix tailwind, not necessarily more lending activity; one that grew loan volume while NIM shrank is trading margin for growth.

## What to check alongside it

- **Is it reported GAAP or on a fully taxable-equivalent (FTE) basis?** Some banks report NIM adjusted to make tax-exempt municipal bond income comparable to taxable income, which nudges the number up slightly. The filing will say which.
- **Total loans and deposits.** A widening NIM on a shrinking loan book is a different story than a widening NIM on a growing one — see the bank's own [net interest income and margin figures](/learn/revenue-vs-net-income) alongside loan and deposit growth.
- **Efficiency ratio and charge-offs.** A high NIM funded by riskier, higher-rate loans can come with higher loan losses later; the [efficiency ratio and net charge-off ratio](/learn/gaap-vs-adjusted-eps) on the same report show whether that trade-off is showing up yet.

NIM is specific to lenders — insurers, brokers, and regular companies don't report it, because they don't fund assets with deposits and borrowed money the way a bank does.
